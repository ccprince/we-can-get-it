# ADR 0006: The map is a sparse gated graph, authored as motifs and drawn per campaign

**Status:** accepted, 2026-10-22
**Context:** `docs/design-overview.md` §4 chunk 3 (the question this settles), §5, §6;
`docs/prototype-findings.md` §2.1, §2.2, §2.5; ADR 0001, ADR 0003, ADR 0004, ADR 0005

## Context

ADR 0005 left the map as **the blocking question** and handed it a specific job: decision 9 made route
length in days a property of the topology, so the map is where *a jump that can be wasted* and ADR 0004's
**distance** axis actually get built.

Two findings carry all the pressure here. First, **no jump was ever wasted** in the prototype, and that
single fact is what left the dump, the stock race and the split inert through eight hand-played runs
(findings §2.1); five effectively fully-connected locations could not produce an unproductive journey.
Second, the most-played shape in the game was never designed: the **outbound fill at an intermediate
stop** — at A, bound for C, stopping at B — and *that shape is topology* (findings §2.1, overview §4).
The galaxy answered "what should I do here?" far better than "what should I do next?", and route-shaped
sub-goals were to be designed deliberately rather than left to fall out of the map.

Against that, ADR 0004 decision 18 had already made the map **variable**: which locations exist and how
they connect is drawn at campaign start from a reserve larger than one campaign can see. So this chunk
had to be both deliberately designed and freshly rolled, which is the tension the decisions below resolve
by separating what is authored from what is placed.

Settled on 2026-10-22 in five rounds of questions. Every number this ADR mentions is explicitly a
measurement, per CLAUDE.md.

## Decision

### The shape of the map

**1. Travel is along authored lanes, not free flight.** The map is a **sparse graph**, not a complete one
with a distance matrix over it. A **lane** is the only way between the two locations it joins. The
prototype's fully-connected five is precisely the arrangement that cannot produce an intermediate stop:
where A→C is always available, B is visited only when the detour happens to be cheap, which is a tuning
accident rather than a design. Sparseness is what makes a place *on the way*, and it is therefore the
source of the outbound fill, the detour and the wasted jump.

**2. A location's identity is authored; its position is generated.** Name, voice, prose, and what the
place is *about* are written by hand and drawn from a reserve with a hidden subset, exactly as ADR 0004
decision 18 already does for people, goods and patrons — generated place-names would cost the prose that
makes landing somewhere worth the stop, and a **tell** (findings §2.4) depends on that prose. Where a
location *sits in the graph* is rolled per campaign. The leak this accepts is "Valhalla is a mining
world, and Ines may be there"; the leak it prevents is "Valhalla is three days from Kestrel, behind a
chokepoint" — which is the whole of what this chunk contributes, and the facts ADR 0004 decision 19 most
wants re-rolled. Variability in *who is drawn for a location* does the rest of the work.

**3. The map is generated by assembling authored motifs, under validated invariants.** A **motif** is an
authored topology fragment with a named shape: a **spur** that must be flown out and back, a
**chokepoint** every route through a region passes, two clusters a long haul apart, a ring that offers a
long way round, a system of three worlds each a day from the others and several from their neighbours.
Generation from raw parameters — node count, branching factor — would produce *a* graph and leave the
shapes findings §2.1 cares about to luck. The invariants (at least one chokepoint, at least so many
spurs, the far cluster no nearer than so far) are checked at load. The grammar of the map is thus fixed
and learnable across campaigns while its facts are not, which is the same move ADR 0005 decision 17 made
with **schedules**.

**4. Motif slots are typed in tag vocabulary, and some are untyped.** A motif's slots name a *class* of
location — an extractor at the spur end, a processor at the junction, a market at the chokepoint — stated
in the **tags** that already exist, and the validator fails a content set whose reserve cannot fill every
slot of every motif. This gives trade a *direction* over the topology: raw out of the spur, finished out
of the junction, so the intermediate stop stops being a coincidence of who happens to want what. Several
authored locations can fill any slot and some are hidden this campaign, which is the guard against
sameness; and a slot may be deliberately **untyped**, so the occasional illogical place sits in the middle
of a legible region and reading the grammar stays a judgement rather than a lookup.

**5. The chart is common knowledge from day one.** Which locations exist, what lanes join them and what
each lane costs are published, always-true facts — the same tier as **schedules**, and part of the
galaxy's grammar. A route's cost is "a promise rather than an estimate" (ADR 0005 decision 9) precisely so
that a tight deadline is a *reading* of the galaxy rather than a gamble on the vacuum, which is ADR 0004
decision 7's epistemic bet; a map discovered by flying it would make the first third of a campaign an
exploration game and every early deadline a wager on an unknown chart.

**6. A location is atomic, and "system" is a name rather than a mechanism.** One node, one action menu, no
interior distance — the same move ADR 0005 decision 1 made for the day. Shops, berths and people are
content hanging off a single node (chunks 5 and 6). Locations may share a **system** name, and the chart
draws them together, but the grouping carries no mechanics: interior geography would be a second travel
system on a second scale. Every lane therefore costs **at least one day**. A zero-day hop would re-create
the multi-shop location this decision rejects, and since trade is free within the day (ADR 0005
decision 5) it would amount to a free shopping trip.

**7. Lanes are symmetric.** One cost, the same both ways. Per-lane direction doubles what every lane on
the chart must say and spends the thing ADR 0005 bought with a regular calendar — route arithmetic a
player can do in their head while judging whether a deadline is makeable. A one-way shape, if it ever
earns its place, is cheaper to get from a motif (a ring whose long way round is the alternative to a
chokepoint) than from a second number on every lane.

### The cost of distance

**8. A lane carries one authored number: its distance.** Not days, not a price. Days and fare are both
derived, which keeps one number per lane on the chart and in the content.

**9. The ship converts distance into days.** `days = ceil(distance / speed)`, minimum one (decision 6).
**Range** is the largest distance the ship can fly in one lane; **speed** is distance per day; **hold** is
cargo. These are three independent purchases and not a ship ladder: ADR 0004 decision 15 makes reach an
*enabler bought with credits* rather than a progression track of its own, and a ladder would force bundles.

**10. The fare is distance times one galaxy-wide rate, quoted and paid at departure.** ADR 0005
decision 14 rejected a **baseline** burn — a cost that ticks whether or not the player acts. A fare for a
journey the player chose is not that, and it buys the second of findings §2.1's two named levers: *a jump
that costs more than a turn*. A wasted journey now costs days **and** credits, deadheading is a visible
loss, and reach has an economic dimension as well as a temporal one. The conditions that keep it clear of
what ADR 0005 rejected: it is **quoted before departure**, so it is a priced decision rather than a meter;
**paid at departure**, so nothing ever accrues against the player, matching the prepayment logic of
standing arrangements; and **zero while the player sits still**, so waiting costs only days.

Note that a faster ship cuts **days but not fare** — distance is distance — so speed and money are
genuinely different purchases.

**11. The fiction is gates and lanes: the fare is gate access, the range is a ship rating, and the
crossing still takes days.** A lane has a **gate** at each end; the fare is access to it. This is the
fiction because the obvious alternative — fuel bought per journey, with the tank as the ceiling on the
purchase — cannot survive the leftover case: a tank of twenty, a first lane costing twelve, a second
costing six, and no honest reason to pay again today. Gate access has no such residue, and it pays for
three other things at once. It answers *why* A→C cannot simply be flown (decision 1): there is no lane.
It makes symmetry a property of a gate pair rather than an assertion (decision 7). And it gives a lane's
closure an **author**, which findings §2.2's central result makes load-bearing: change the player can
attribute reads as a living world at any volume, and change they cannot reads as dice at any volume.

The condition on the fiction: **a gate is an on-ramp, not a teleport.** The lane is days long and is
played day by day, or ADR 0005 decisions 10 and 11 both fall — transit as a place, its thin menu, being
out of contact, the arrival digest, and distance costing knowledge as well as days.

**12. Mail must always pay its own fare.** ADR 0005 decision 18 made **mail** the floor under the promise
that being broke costs days and never the campaign. A fare turns that promise into a content invariant:
at every location, some mail offer's advance must cover the fare of the lane it travels, checked at load.

**13. Tolls are events, not geography.** The baseline rate is uniform. A **surcharge on a particular
gate** is a change-budget event running the three beats of ADR 0005 decision 16 — and that ADR already
names a tariff dispute as its example of a rumour. An authored per-lane tariff is a static fact the player
memorises once; a toll dispute is something they read, route around and watch resolve, so the same fiction
feeds the organ that carries aliveness instead of the chart's static data.

### Reach, gating and the draw

**14. Range is the only gate on the map; acts move the patrons, not the walls.** ADR 0004's "clearing an
act unlocks further places" is implemented as patrons whose commissions lie further off, never as a region
locked by act. A region locked by standing is a wall the player can see and cannot act on; a region beyond
baseline range is the same wall with a **price** on it, which makes the first range upgrade a remembered
moment rather than an errand. It also keeps standing doing exactly one job (ADR 0004 decision 13: which
commissions are offered, never the player's capability) and keeps the map from becoming a second standing
system.

**15. Everything drawn is visitable; the reserve is what a campaign does not see.** No in-campaign region
locking and nothing permanently out of bounds: what one campaign misses is what was never drawn — undrawn
locations and hidden characters, per ADR 0004 decision 18. Overview §5's "how much does one campaign see"
is therefore the **draw size**, a measurement. The invariant to validate is that the drawn subgraph is
**connected at baseline range**, or a campaign can open with an unreachable component.

**16. The campaign starts at a market hub.** The generator guarantees a start with three or more
neighbours inside baseline range, at least one of them a spur, and validates it. A start on a spur makes
the first decision "fly out, or fly out", and worse, makes the **outbound fill** unavailable until the
player has already learned the game — the shape the prototype proved most-played is the one the opening
hour should teach.

### Knowledge and change on the map

**17. A location's standing character is published; its current contents are not.** The kinds of thing
traded at a place — stated in the vocabulary of **tags**, carried by the **trade guide** — are knowable
from day one, so a player who accepts an order on the starting world always knows where to *start
looking*. The specific **lot**, its price, its quantity and who is there to want it are discovered, carry
an **as-of date**, and are corrected by landing. The player therefore never knows whether the thing is
actually there at a price they can pay, which is ADR 0004 decision 7's bet, and the published tier never
answers the player's riddle (overview §4).

**18. Lanes carry rumours and notices, and they bind only at departure.** ADR 0005 decision 16's three
beats were "pointed at the map": a lane closes, slows, or backs up at a chokepoint. This is how the map
earns a place in the change budget rather than being inert scenery. A notice changes **departures** and
never a journey already underway — the player who left yesterday flies the cost they were quoted — so
decision 9's *fixed at departure* survives a live map, and the one thing ADR 0005 calls unforgivable, an
unannounced lost day, cannot happen.

**19. A place never leaves the chart; a lane can close, dated and usually bounded.** The map's version of
findings §2.2's absence result — the buyer leaves the market, not the want. A location can go dark, close
to trade, or be unreachable because its gate is shut, and it stays on the chart as last known with its
as-of date. A closure states its reopening wherever the fiction allows one, because an open-ended closure
is the map's version of an unannounced lost day; the rare unbounded closure is still dated at its *start*,
which is the promise ADR 0005 actually made, and the player's answer — buy range, go round, wait it out —
is a real decision.

**20. The chart is a first-class reading surface, and it does the arithmetic.** Findings §2.5: every
system the design wants the player to reason about needs its own reading surface. This chunk hands the
player four things per lane — distance, days *for their ship*, fare, and whether their range reaches at
all — plus any rumour or notice sitting on it. So the **chart** joins the trade guide, contacts and the
ledger in chunk 8, and it shows the converted numbers rather than the raw ones: not "distance 14" but
"four days, 140 credits, within range", and "beyond your range" on the lanes that are not flyable.

## Rejected alternatives

- **Free flight between any two locations**, with cost derived from position. Decision 1. It is the
  prototype's arrangement, and findings §2.1 traces three inert mechanics and the missing intermediate
  stop directly to it.
- **A map generated from parameters** — node count, branching factor — over an authored location pool.
  Decision 3. It hands the one genuine design decision in this chunk to a generator, against the finding
  that route-shaped sub-goals must be designed deliberately.
- **Generated location names and prose.** Decision 2. It buys variability in the one dimension where
  authored writing is doing irreplaceable work, and a tag's **tell** lives in that writing.
- **A map discovered by flying it.** Decision 5. It makes every early deadline a gamble on an unknown
  chart, which is the random uncertainty ADR 0004 decision 7 rejects in favour of an epistemic one.
- **A two-tier graph of systems containing locations.** Decision 6. A system is a graph with more nodes;
  the tier would add a second travel scale and a second unit on the chart for nothing the cost structure
  cannot express.
- **Zero-day hops within a system.** Decision 6. Since trade is free within the day, a free hop is a free
  shopping trip, and the cluster collapses back into the atomic multi-shop location.
- **Asymmetric lane costs.** Decision 7. Two numbers on every lane, spending the in-the-head arithmetic
  ADR 0005 bought with a regular calendar; a motif gets the same effect where it is actually wanted.
- **No money cost for travel at all.** Decision 10. The first answer here, and wrong: it over-read ADR
  0005 decision 14, which rejected an *unchosen baseline* burn, not a priced cost of a chosen journey —
  and it threw away the cheapest version of findings §2.1's second lever.
- **Fuel as a tracked consumable** — a level, a refuel action, a price per unit varying by location.
  Decision 11. A second commodity with its own arbitrage, a meter on every reading surface, a chore at
  every landing, and a stranding risk of exactly the kind ADR 0004 and ADR 0005 both forbid. Fuel is also
  explicitly **not cargo**: competing with the hold would be the same second economy by another door.
- **Fuel bought per journey at departure, with the tank as the ceiling on the purchase.** The fiction
  before decision 11, killed by the leftover case stated there.
- **Authored per-lane tariffs or regional rates.** Decision 13. A static fact memorised once, where the
  event version is read, routed around and watched.
- **Regions locked by act.** Decision 14. A wall with no price on it, and a second job for standing.

## Consequences

- **The blocking question moves off the map.** Overview §6 item 3 is half done: topology is settled, and
  what remains before the first playable milestone are the **four upstream parameters** of §5 — the tag
  structure, how much of the galaxy one campaign sees, and the ratios that follow. The tag structure is
  now the next question, and decision 4 has just given it a second customer: tags now type the map's
  slots as well as matching goods to wants.
- **ADR 0005's "a journey costs a route-dependent number of days" is now concrete**: the route-dependence
  is a lane's authored **distance**, converted by the ship's **speed** (decisions 8, 9).
- **`jump` is recovered as a word.** It means the traversal of one lane, which is the sense GLOSSARY.md
  already kept; ADR 0005's retirement of its *unit of time* sense stands.
- **The validator gains jobs** (chunk 9): every motif slot fillable from the reserve (decision 4); the
  calendar-style structural checks extended to the drawn graph's connectivity at baseline range
  (decision 15); the start-node guarantee (decision 16); mail's advance covering its lane's fare
  (decision 12); the motif invariants themselves (decision 3).
- **The content pipeline gains two kinds of content it did not have**: **motifs** with typed slots, and a
  location's published **standing character** in tag vocabulary. The cross-product tool needs to be able
  to show a writer which locations can fill which slots.
- **A gate operator is new content.** Decision 11's fiction implies somebody who maintains gates, sets
  tariffs and can close one. This is the cost of the fiction, accepted because it is what makes a lane's
  closure attributable (findings §2.2) and a toll dispute sayable (decision 13).
- **Chunk 8 gains the chart** as a named surface (decision 20), and the trade guide gains two things that
  are not current facts: a location's standing character (decision 17) and lane rumours (decision 18).
- **Reach is three purchases, not a ladder** (decision 9), which is what ADR 0004 decision 15 and 16 were
  already pointing at, and the map is where the first of them — range — is felt.
- **The numbers this hands to measurement**, none of them decidable here: the distance scale and the
  spread between the shortest and longest lane; the fare rate; baseline range, speed and hold; the draw
  size and how many motifs a campaign is assembled from; how many spurs and chokepoints the invariants
  demand; how long a lane closure runs; what a toll surcharge adds.

## Open questions this deliberately leaves

- **Research as an action, and the on-board computer** (parked during this session). In transit the player
  is out of contact, so research cannot return current facts about a distant market without deleting the
  cost of distance; the shape that survives is **inference, not news** — working over the published
  grammar plus the player's own dated observations to yield **leads**, never a current fact, while current
  remote facts are bought in contact or through an arrangement that reaches the ship. Two constraints
  recorded for whoever picks it up: its output must not be something the player could derive with pencil
  and paper outside game time, which means it has to depend on the ship's instruments and on what the
  player has actually seen; and it must narrow the search without ever terminating it (overview §4).
- **Whether a lane closure can disconnect the drawn map mid-campaign.** Decision 15 validates connectivity
  at baseline range *at campaign start*; decision 19 lets a lane close later. Whether a closure may isolate
  a component, and for how long, is unsettled — the tension is between a closure that really bites and the
  rule that being broke or stuck costs days and never the campaign.
- **Whether the gate operator has any mechanics**, or stays a voice that explains closures and tolls.
- **Transit events on a lane**, inherited whole from ADR 0005: whether an event may ever move a journey's
  length, which decision 10 here still provisionally says no to, and whether one may take cargo.
